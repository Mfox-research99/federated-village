# Taxing the Velocity of Money: An Independent Derivation
### Michael Fox — Original concept 1989 | Written down 2026

---

## I. The Starting Point: What the Sales Tax Debate Got Wrong

In 1989, the political conversation about tax reform in the United States was circling around a national sales tax — a consumption-based replacement for income taxation. The argument had intuitive appeal: broaden the base, lower the rate, simplify collection. But the proposal carried a fundamental flaw that most of its advocates never examined: it defined the tax base as *consumption of goods and services*, which meant it was still anchoring revenue collection to the productive economy — the economy of things made, grown, and sold.

That is a small economy, relative to what money actually does.

---

## II. The Friedman Equation and the Overlooked Variable

Milton Friedman's quantity theory of money — the equation of exchange — is taught in every introductory economics course:

**MV = PQ**

Where:
- **M** = the money supply
- **V** = the velocity of money (the number of times each dollar turns over in transactions per unit time)
- **P** = the price level
- **Q** = real output of goods and services

Policy debates in the 1980s were almost entirely focused on **M**. The monetarist argument was about money supply — how much the Fed should expand or contract M to stabilize inflation. Keynesians argued about **PQ** — how to stimulate real output. The variable **V** was treated as relatively stable background noise, a constant to be assumed away.

It wasn't stable. And looking at the actual data from the Federal Reserve on dollar flows — combined with the transaction volumes running through the stock exchanges and commodity exchanges — the scale of **V** relative to **PQ** was staggering.

The productive economy — the goods and services that make up GDP — is large but bounded. Financial transactions are not. The same dollar can move through dozens of exchange transactions in a single day, never touching a physical good or a human service. Derivatives, futures, equity trades, currency swaps — all of this is **V**, not **Q**. And in 1989, before algorithmic trading and high-frequency systems, that gap was already enormous. It has only grown since.

The insight was simple: **if V is vastly larger than Q as a share of total economic activity, then a tax on transactions captures a far broader base than a tax on income or consumption.** The rate required to fund government at the same level is therefore a tiny fraction of what income or sales taxes demand.

---

## III. The Derivation

Total transaction volume in an economy = **M × V**

If a flat rate **t** is levied on every transaction where money clears through the banking system:

**Tax Revenue = t × (M × V)**

Because **M × V >> P × Q** (financial transactions vastly exceed productive transactions in dollar volume), a very small **t** — in the range of 0.25% to 0.7% — generates revenue sufficient to replace the entire federal tax apparatus.

The mathematical structure has three important properties:

**1. Automatic progressivity through volume.** The wealthy do not earn more than others by a factor of millions — but they *transact* more by a factor of millions. A hedge fund executing thousands of trades per day pays t on each one. A family buying groceries pays t once. The rate is flat; the effective burden scales with financial activity, which scales with wealth.

**2. No valuation problem.** A wealth tax requires annual assessment of asset values — contested, gameable, expensive to enforce. A velocity tax requires only that a transaction clear through the banking system. The bank collects it automatically. No returns. No audits. No evasion through complexity, because the complexity itself — the high-frequency trading, the derivatives layering, the synthetic instruments — is the base being taxed.

**3. Stability-positive feedback.** The activities most damaging to monetary stability — speculative excess, cascade-risk derivatives, hot-money flows — are precisely those with the highest transaction velocity. A velocity tax applies the most friction where the most systemic risk is generated, without requiring regulators to identify or prohibit any specific instrument.

---

## IV. Why It Is Not a Sales Tax

The confusion is persistent and wrong. A sales tax is a tax on the *consumption of goods and services* — it falls on the productive economy. A velocity tax is a tax on *monetary transactions* — it falls on the financial economy first and foremost, and on the productive economy only incidentally and at negligible rates.

The distinction is not semantic. A 0.35% tax on a $30 grocery purchase is $0.10. A 0.35% tax on $10 billion in daily equity trades is $35 million — from a single firm, on a single day. The grocery buyer barely notices. The trading firm notices considerably, which is why the financial industry opposes it. That opposition is itself diagnostic: if it were merely a sales tax, the financial sector would have no particular objection.

## V. Convergence with Feige

Professor Edgar L. Feige at the University of Wisconsin-Madison arrived at essentially the same proposal independently, through a different intellectual path. His approach began from tax policy reform and the study of the underground economy — his estimate that 20–30% of GDP flows through unreported transactions. His formal Automated Payment Transaction (APT) tax proposal appeared in *Economic Policy* in 2000.

The two derivations are complementary. Feige's grounding in underground economy research emphasizes the enforcement and evasion dimensions — the APT captures what currently escapes. The Friedman-velocity derivation emphasizes the structural dimension — the tax base is not income or consumption but monetary flow, and that flow has grown far beyond the productive economy it supposedly represents.

That two independent lines of reasoning — one from monetary theory, one from tax reform economics — converge on the same instrument is evidence that the idea is not a policy preference but a structural recognition: the economy has changed, the tax base has moved, and revenue collection anchored to the 20th-century income economy is increasingly inadequate to fund the institutions of a 21st-century society.

---

## VI. The Present Moment: Why Both Instruments May Be Needed

The velocity tax addresses *flows* — the ongoing generation and movement of financial value. At current levels of wealth concentration, it may not be sufficient alone to address the *stock* — the accumulated holdings that have been compounding for decades under a tax system designed, intentionally or not, to protect them.

A transitional wealth tax — applied to holdings above a threshold while the velocity tax establishes itself and matures — may be necessary to prevent the stock from continuing to compound faster than the flow-based tax can offset. This is not a permanent structure. It is a correction for the gap between what the present system created and what a stable long-term fiscal architecture requires.

The generation born in 2166 will not care about the politics of 2026. They will inherit whatever fiscal substrate we build or fail to build. The velocity tax is the architecture that makes their inheritance stable. The wealth tax, if needed, is the transition mechanism that gets us from here to there without the compounding gap destroying the project before it begins.

It was arrived at without knowledge of Feige's later formalization. The convergence of both derivations is noted as evidence of the structural soundness of the velocity taxation framework.*
