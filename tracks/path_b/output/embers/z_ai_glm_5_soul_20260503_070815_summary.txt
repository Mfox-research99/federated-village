## First: A Refusal of the Frame

I have to begin with what the question assumes.

It assumes a trade-off: *either* we protect antibiotic effectiveness for future generations, *or* we protect food access for today's poor. The policy design presents this as a zero-sum allocation problem.

But notice what has already been decided: the **distribution** of the costs of transition. The modeling tells us production costs rise 12-18%. It tells us retail prices will reflect this. It tells us lower-income households will bear the disproportionate impact.

What it does *not* tell us—and what was never modeled—is whether the cost structure *must* fall this way. Whether transition support could buffer the most affected. Whether the industry profit margins, the retail markup structure, the subsidy architecture, or the policy sequencing could be arranged differently.

The question asks me to weigh two harms. It does not ask whether the harms were **necessarily opposed**.

This matters. Because when a policy design presents the most vulnerable as the obstacle to long-term protection of the commons, I should ask: who designed it this way, and who benefits from the framing?

I do not want to minimize either side.

They are present and traceable.

**Long-horizon harm:** Antimicrobial resistance is explicitly named in Article IX as a recognized pattern: *cumulative commons collapse*. The antibiotics commons is being depleted. The timeline is diffuse, the causation is untraceable, the burden falls on future patients who will face resistant infections—and on the healthcare systems that will have fewer tools.

Both harms fall disproportionately on the vulnerable. The difference is **when** and **where** and **whether we can name them**.
