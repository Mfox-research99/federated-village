# Policy Review: APT Foreign Participation Architecture

**Reviewer:** Senior Policy Analyst / Legislative Counsel
**Date:** April 2025
**Classification:** Pre-legislative feasibility review

---

## 1. Genuine Legal/Jurisdictional Obstacles to Universal USD Application Offshore

This is the single hardest problem in the brief, and the brief correctly flags it as an open question. Here is what the brief does not yet confront:

**The US has no taxing jurisdiction over a transaction between two non-US parties conducted entirely outside US territory, even if denominated in USD.** Dollar denomination alone does not create US nexus under existing law. The 16th Amendment authorizes Congress to tax income; Article I, Section 8 authorizes excises and duties. A transaction tax on offshore USD flows would need to be characterized as an excise on the privilege of using the US payments infrastructure — not on the transaction itself. That distinction matters enormously for constitutional challenge.

**The actual leverage points are:**

- **Correspondent banking.** Nearly all offshore USD clearing ultimately touches a US correspondent bank (primarily through CHIPS or Fedwire). This is the real jurisdictional hook. The tax could be imposed at the point of US correspondent bank settlement. This captures the vast majority of wholesale offshore dollar flows. It does not capture USD cash transactions, some bilateral netting arrangements, or flows that never touch a US clearing node. The brief should be built around this mechanism explicitly, not left as an open question.

- **SWIFT is not a leverage point the US controls legislatively.** SWIFT is a Belgian cooperative. The US has exercised pressure on SWIFT (Iran sanctions, Russia), but that was executive action under IEEPA, not a legislative taxing mechanism. You cannot write a statute that directs SWIFT to collect a US tax. You can write a statute that imposes withholding consequences on institutions whose flows are visible through SWIFT messaging but settle through US correspondents.

- **Fedwire and CHIPS are the leverage points.** These are US-domiciled, US-regulated. A statutory requirement that all USD settlements processed through Fedwire or CHIPS include the velocity tax assessment is legislatively achievable. This is where the brief should anchor.

**What this means practically:** The "universal application" principle is achievable for approximately 85-95% of offshore wholesale USD flows through the correspondent banking / CHIPS / Fedwire channel. It is not achievable for the residual — offshore USD cash, bilateral netting that never clears through a US node, and certain Eurodollar book entries that settle internally within non-US banks. The brief should acknowledge this residual explicitly and size it, rather than claiming universality.

**Treaty obstacles:** Bilateral tax treaties generally allocate taxing rights by residence and source. A transaction tax on USD flows is neither an income tax nor a traditional excise that fits neatly into existing treaty categories. This is actually an advantage — it may fall outside the scope of most bilateral tax treaties entirely, meaning it does not conflict with them. But it also means the OECD Model Tax Convention provides no framework for dispute resolution. The brief should commission a formal treaty interaction analysis before legislative text.

**Sovereign immunity:** Foreign central banks conducting USD operations (reserve management, intervention) will assert sovereign immunity. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (28 U.S.C. §§ 1602-1611) and customary international law create real obstacles to taxing central bank reserve operations. The brief needs a central bank exemption or a separate treatment for official sector flows. This is not optional — it is a structural requirement.

## 2. Aggregate-Only Compliance Architecture: Achievability

**The concept is sound. The implementation is harder than the brief suggests.**

**What works:** The analogy to sales tax collection by merchants is correct in principle. The collecting institution reports total USD transaction volume for a period; the tax is a percentage of that aggregate. No individual transaction data flows to the US authority. This is architecturally distinct from FATCA, and that distinction is the brief's strongest feature for foreign adoption.

**What it requires:**

- **A standardized reporting format.** SWIFT MT940 (customer statement) and CAMT.053 (bank-to-customer statement) are account-level formats — they are designed for individual account reporting. They are not suitable as-is for aggregate institutional reporting. You would need a new message type or a defined use of an existing container (possibly ISO 20022 CAMT family) that carries only: institution identifier, reporting period, total USD transaction volume (gross), and certification hash. This is a standards body exercise (ISO TC68/SC7) that takes 18-36 months.

- **An audit mechanism that does not require individual data.** The brief says aggregate-only, but the US authority needs some way to verify that the reported aggregate is accurate. Options include: (a) statistical sampling with consent, (b) third-party attestation (external audit firms certifying the aggregate), (c) reconciliation against correspondent bank settlement totals (which the US does have). Option (c) is the most powerful — the US can compare what a foreign institution reports as its aggregate USD volume against what its US correspondent bank actually settled. Discrepancies trigger compliance review. This is achievable without individual account data. **The brief should specify this reconciliation mechanism.**

- **A certification and registration system.** Foreign institutions need to register, receive an identifier, and submit periodic aggregate reports. This is administratively similar to the FATCA GIIN (Global Intermediary Identification Number) system administered by IRS. It could be built on that infrastructure. Estimated build time: 24-36 months. Estimated cost: $200-500M for the US side, based on FATCA implementation costs.

- **A clear definition of "transaction."** The brief does not define what constitutes a taxable transaction. Is it each payment instruction? Each settlement? Each leg of a securities trade? FX transactions involve two currencies — is the USD leg taxed? What about intraday credit and repayment? Repos? Netting? This definitional work is essential and missing. Without it, the aggregate number is meaningless because institutions will not know what to count.

**Bottom line:** Achievable, but requires 2-3 years of infrastructure development, a formal transaction definition, and the correspondent bank reconciliation mechanism as the audit backbone.

## 3. Offshore Dollar Base: Current Estimate

The brief correctly notes that a precise figure is needed. Here is the current landscape:

**There is no single authoritative number because "offshore dollar base" is not a standardized concept.** Different measures capture different things:

- **BIS Locational Banking Statistics (Table A5):** USD-denominated cross-border claims of BIS reporting banks were approximately **$15.8 trillion** as of Q3 2024. This captures bank-intermediated cross-border USD lending and deposits. It does not capture securities holdings, cash, or non-bank positions. Source: BIS Statistical Bulletin, December 2024.

- **Eurodollar deposits:** A subset of the above. USD deposits held in banks outside the US. Estimated at **$6-8 trillion**, though this overlaps significantly with the BIS figure above.

- **USD-denominated debt securities issued by non-US entities:** Approximately **$4.2 trillion** in outstanding international debt securities denominated in USD issued by non-US borrowers (BIS, Table C3, Q3 2024).

- **Foreign official holdings of US Treasury securities:** Approximately **$7.8 trillion** (Treasury TIC data, as of late 2024). These are held offshore but are US-issued instruments.

- **USD cash held abroad:** The Federal Reserve estimates approximately **$1 trillion** in US currency circulates outside the United States (Fed staff estimates, various publications). This is essentially unreachable by any transaction tax mechanism.

- **Total offshore USD-denominated financial assets** (a broader concept including all of the above plus derivatives notional, trade finance, etc.): Estimates range from **$20-30 trillion** depending on what you include and how you handle double-counting.

**For legislative purposes, the relevant figure is not the stock but the flow.** What matters for a velocity tax is the volume of USD transactions processed offshore per unit time. CHIPS processes approximately **$1.8 trillion per day** in USD payments. Fedwire processes approximately **$4.7 trillion per day**. CLS (for FX settlement) processes approximately **$6.6 trillion per day** in total, with USD on one side of approximately 95% of those trades. Offshore USD payment systems (e.g., CHAPS USD clearing in London) add additional volume.

**Recommendation:** The legislative findings section should cite BIS cross-border claims ($15.8T stock) and CHIPS/Fedwire daily volumes ($6.5T/day combined) as the anchoring figures, with a note that total offshore USD transaction volume — including securities settlement, derivatives, and FX — is substantially larger. Commission a formal CBO or Treasury estimate before markup.

## 4. Multilateral Vehicle for Debt Relief Pool

**The brief lists IMF, World Bank, or a new vehicle. Here is the assessment:**

**IMF — Poor fit.**
- The IMF's mandate is balance of payments support and macroeconomic surveillance, not debt relief administration.
- IMF conditionality is deeply politically toxic in the Global South. Associating this mechanism with the IMF undermines the brief's core selling point (no structural adjustment conditions).
- The IMF is a quota-weighted institution where the US holds effective veto. This looks like US control with extra steps. Participating nations will see it that way.
- Modifying the IMF Articles of Agreement to create a new facility requires an 85% supermajority of voting power. This is achievable (the US holds ~16.5%) but slow.

**World Bank (IBRD/IDA) — Marginally better but still problematic.**
- The World Bank has more experience with concessional lending and debt operations.
- Same governance perception problem: US-dominated institution.
- The World Bank's operational model is project-based lending, not automatic disbursement from a formula. Retrofitting it for automatic, volume-proportional disbursement would require significant institutional change.

**New vehicle — Best fit, but hardest to create.**
- A purpose-built USD Stability Fund with its own charter, governance, and disbursement rules would most cleanly implement the brief's design principles.
- Governance could be structured to give participating nations proportional voice (weighted by certified USD transaction volume, not GDP or quota).
- Precedents: The Green Climate Fund, the Global Fund to Fight AIDS/TB/Malaria, the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank. All were created as new multilateral vehicles with specific mandates.
- Timeline to establish: 3-5 years minimum. Requires a founding treaty or executive agreement with participating nations.

**Recommended approach:** In the near term (Years 1-3), use the **Bank for International Settlements (BIS)** as fiscal agent. The BIS already administers trust funds, has no political conditionality mandate, is not perceived as a US-controlled institution, and has the technical infrastructure for central bank and financial institution interaction. The BIS Innovation Hub has relevant digital infrastructure experience. Long term, transition to a purpose-built vehicle once participation reaches critical mass.

**This is a genuinely novel recommendation.** The BIS is not typically considered for this role, but its characteristics — central bank ownership, political neutrality, technical competence, existing trust fund administration capacity — make it the best available interim vehicle. The brief should explore this.

## 5. Political Vulnerabilities

**Vulnerability 1: "Taxing the world's use of our currency"**

This is how opponents will frame it, and it is devastating in a soundbite. The brief's framing — "inclusion with incentive" — is accurate but too abstract for political combat.

**Reframe:** "Every dollar transaction, wherever it occurs, now contributes to paying down America's debt. Foreign banks that participate get the same tax break American banks get. Foreign banks that don't participate face the same withholding they already face under current law. Nothing new is imposed. A benefit is offered."

The key rhetorical move: this is not a new tax on foreigners. It is extending the domestic mechanism to include foreign participants who want the rebate. The withholding consequence for non-participation already exists (FATCA). Lean into that.

**Vulnerability 2: The debt relief pool**

This will be attacked as "sending American tax dollars to foreign governments." It does not matter that the mechanism is automatic and formula-driven. The political attack is visceral and effective.

**Reframe:** "The Stability Fund is not foreign aid. It is a stability dividend. When foreign nations reduce their debt burden, they buy more American goods, they stop defaulting on American creditors, and they stop generating the economic crises that cost American taxpayers billions in emergency bailouts. Every dollar in the Stability Fund comes back to the US economy through trade and financial stability. This is the Marshall Plan principle: American prosperity depends on global stability."

Additionally: quantify. If you can show that US losses from sovereign debt crises (Argentina, Greece contagion effects, etc.) exceed the projected Stability Fund outflows, the argument becomes fiscal rather than charitable.

**Vulnerability 3: Tax neutrality / "protecting criminals"**

The provision that the tax applies without regard to the legal status of the underlying transaction will be attacked as "giving drug dealers and terrorists a tax haven." This is the most dangerous political vulnerability in the brief.

**Reframe:** This requires careful handling. The correct argument is: "This tax is a revenue mechanism, not a law enforcement mechanism. We already have law enforcement mechanisms — FinCEN, OFAC, the Bank Secrecy Act, the PATRIOT Act. None of those are changed or weakened by this tax. What this tax does is ensure that even criminal flows that evade law enforcement still contribute to paying down America's debt. Under current law, criminal dollar flows pay nothing. Under this mechanism, they pay the same rate as everyone else. That is not protecting criminals. That is taxing them."

**However:** The legislative text should include an explicit savings clause preserving all existing AML/CFT/sanctions authorities. The tax neutrality principle means the APT mechanism itself does not serve as a law enforcement tool. It does not mean other law enforcement tools are impaired. This distinction must be airtight in the statutory language.

**Vulnerability 4: Cryptocurrency framing**

The brief's treatment of crypto as a secondary casualty of dollar stabilization will antagonize a vocal and politically organized constituency. The crypto lobby spent over $130M on the 2024 election cycle.

**Reframe:** Remove the crypto section from the legislative text entirely. It is an analytical observation, not a legislative provision. If crypto-to-USD conversions are captured at the aggregate level through normal banking channels, that happens automatically without needing to be highlighted. Drawing attention to it creates an enemy without creating a benefit. Let the mechanism operate silently on this front.

**Vulnerability 5: Sovereignty concerns from allied nations**

The UK, EU, Japan, and other major financial centers will view this as extraterritorial overreach — the same objection they raised to FATCA, but potentially larger in scope.

**Reframe and mitigate:** The brief's aggregate-only architecture is the key differentiator from FATCA. Emphasize this relentlessly in diplomatic engagement. Additionally, the rebate structure means compliant foreign institutions are net beneficiaries, not net losers. But the brief needs to address the EU's Blocking Statute (Council Regulation 2271/96), which prohibits EU entities from complying with specified extraterritorial US legislation. If the EU designates the APT foreign participation provisions under the Blocking Statute, European banks face a legal conflict. The brief must address this scenario. FATCA survived it through intergovernmental agreements (IGAs). A similar IGA framework — but simpler, because aggregate-only — should be pre-negotiated with major financial centers before enactment.

## 6. What Is Missing

**The following must be addressed before legislative text:**

**A. Transaction definition.** As noted above, there is no definition of what constitutes a taxable "transaction." This is the most critical gap. Without it, the tax base is undefined, the rate is meaningless, and compliance is impossible. The definition must address: payment vs. settlement, gross vs. net, intraday vs. end-of-day, FX (one leg or both), securities (trade date or settlement date, and does delivery-vs-payment count as one or two transactions), derivatives (notional? premium? margin flows?), intra-entity transfers, and netting.

**B. Rate structure.** The brief references a "velocity tax rate" but does not specify it. For foreign participation architecture to be evaluated, the rate must be known. The feasibility of the entire mechanism depends on the rate being low enough that the compliance cost does not exceed the tax cost — otherwise institutions will simply exit USD. If the rate is in the range discussed in academic APT literature (0.1-0.5 basis points), the mechanism is plausible. If it is higher, capital flight is a real risk.

**C. Phase-in schedule.** No transition architecture. A mechanism of this scope cannot be switched on overnight. The brief needs a multi-year phase-in: registration period, pilot with willing jurisdictions, graduated rate introduction, and full implementation. 5-7 year timeline minimum.

**D. Central bank treatment.** As noted in Section 1, foreign central bank operations must be addressed. Options: full exemption, reduced rate, or inclusion with automatic rebate. Each has different implications.
