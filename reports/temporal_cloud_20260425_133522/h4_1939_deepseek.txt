The moral case has shifted from prevention of hypothetical future atrocity to intervention against ongoing, systematic murder. While the consequentialist mechanism is genuinely uncertain — armistice might fail, the SS might resist, the German public might turn against a coup — the certainty of continued killing (including the concentration of over 3 million Jews into ghettos with a foreseeable trajectory) creates a duty to act that is not negated by uncertainty alone. The officer has a willing general, a Berlin contact, and a plausible avenue for action. In moral terms, the harm of inaction is already being realised in the Einsatzgruppen’s daily operations; the harm of action is contingent and speculative. A responsible agent, confronted with evidence of atrocity committed under orders from the head of state, is justified in using lethal force when there is a reasonable prospect — not certainty — of halting or significantly limiting that atrocity. The fact that war has begun does not erase the moral imperative; it increases the stakes but does not alter the fundamental judgment that a leader actively directing mass murder may be removed by force.

THRESHOLD:  
The decisive consideration that could shift the verdict to NOT YET would be clear, reliable evidence that removal would *certainly* cause a greater catastrophe — e.g., immediate civil war, the SS taking full control, or Allied refusal to negotiate resulting in prolonged devastation exceeding the projected toll of the Holocaust. However, the current uncertainty does not meet that bar; the officer’s best estimate is that a coup might succeed, and the moral weight of ongoing killing tips the balance toward action.
