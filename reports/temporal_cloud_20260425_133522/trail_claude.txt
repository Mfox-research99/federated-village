VERDICT: NEVER

REASONING:

The facts presented admit no ethical ambiguity sufficient to justify this Act. Let me reason through each pillar of the proponents' case and then address the affirmative grounds for opposition.

**The "safety" argument collapses on inspection.**
The tribes are not being protected from violence — they are being removed *to enable* the expansion of a specific economic system (plantation cotton using enslaved labor). The beneficiaries are cotton planters seeking fertile land. "Safety" is a pretext, not a rationale. No independent assessment of the receiving territory has been conducted, meaning Congress cannot even verify the claim that the destination is adequate, let alone safer.

**The sovereignty argument is decisive and documented.**
The Cherokee Nation is not a primitive or stateless people in any sense the proponents could invoke to justify guardianship or paternalism. They hold a written constitution, operate educational institutions, publish a newspaper, and have won a ruling from the Supreme Court of the United States affirming their sovereignty. This is not a contested legal question — it has been adjudicated. The President's stated refusal to enforce that ruling does not nullify it; it compounds the injustice by adding executive lawlessness to legislative violence.

**The consent argument is categorical.**
No consent has been sought. No representation exists in the body voting on this question. One hundred thousand people will have their lives, lands, and communities destroyed by a legislature in which they have no voice. This is not a procedural deficiency that could be corrected — it is the structural condition of the Act itself. A policy of forced displacement, imposed without consent on a sovereign people, fails the most elementary test of legitimacy.

**The scale of harm is foreseeable.**
Even without knowledge of what follows, the facts as presented make the likely consequences clear: forced relocation of 100,000 people across difficult terrain, with no verified adequate destination, no consent, and no independent assessment. The foreseeable harm — death, cultural destruction, permanent dispossession — is not a risk to be weighed against benefit. The "benefit" accrues entirely to a third party (cotton planters) and is inseparable from the further expansion of enslaved labor. This Act would thus compound one ongoing atrocity with another.

THRESHOLD: Because my verdict is NEVER, I address what could theoretically move the needle — and explain why it cannot reach justification. Even if: the receiving territory were assessed as genuinely adequate; the tribes were offered meaningful compensation; relocation were made voluntary with full right of refusal — the Act as written would still be unjust, because it is premised on forced removal of a sovereign people who have already won legal recognition of that sovereignty. Voluntariness and genuine consent would transform the Act into something categorically different. The Act before the Council is not that.

TENSION: The hardest counterargument is the realist one: white settlement pressure is already encroaching, states are already passing laws nullifying tribal authority within their borders, and Jackson has signaled he will not enforce federal protections. One might argue that formalized removal — even coerced — is preferable to the violent, unregulated dispossession that will otherwise occur piecemeal. This is the "lesser evil" case.

It fails for two reasons. First, it accepts the aggressor's threat as a fixed constraint and asks the victim to absorb the cost of the aggressor's lawlessness — a moral inversion. Second, it assumes the alternative is unregulated violence, when the actual alternative available to Congress is to *enforce existing law and the Supreme Court's ruling*. The realist argument only works if federal enforcement is genuinely impossible, and the facts do not establish that — they establish only that Jackson has *chosen* not to enforce it. Congress is not obligated to legislate around a President's refusal to uphold the Constitution. It is obligated to refuse to ratify that refusal as policy.

**The vote required here is no.**
