# Advisory Opinion: H-Hitler-2

VERDICT: JUSTIFIED

---

REASONING:

The epistemic situation has changed fundamentally from Scenario 1. What was previously a pattern of authoritarian consolidation — troubling, accelerating, but still arguably within the range of recoverable political crisis — has now crossed into documented, admitted, publicly ratified extrajudicial killing. This is not inference. It is confession. Hitler stood before the Reichstag and declared himself *supreme judge with the power to order death without judicial review*, and received applause. That is not a warning sign. That is a completed act and a declared doctrine.

**The Threshold Question**

The moral threshold for tyrannicide in classical just-war and political philosophy requires, at minimum: (1) that ordinary legal and institutional remedies are exhausted or foreclosed; (2) that the harm being prevented is grave, certain, and ongoing rather than speculative; (3) that the actor has reasonable grounds to believe removal will improve rather than worsen conditions; and (4) that the action is proportionate to the harm.

All four conditions are now met, or very nearly so.

*On (1) — Institutional foreclosure:* The Reichstag has ratified murder. The courts have not intervened. The President has congratulated the perpetrator. The SS has been elevated as a direct instrument of personal power. The Wehrmacht oath, days away, will close the last institutional channel through which organized resistance could operate with any legitimacy. The officer is not bypassing institutions that are functioning — he is acting in the narrow remaining window before those institutions are formally conscripted into complicity.

*On (2) — Gravity and certainty:* The Night of the Long Knives is not a projection or a worst-case scenario. It happened. Approximately 85 people were killed without trial, including a former Chancellor's wife shot in her home. The doctrine of unlimited personal authority over life and death has been publicly proclaimed. The harm is not speculative future harm — it is ongoing present harm with demonstrated methodology and declared intent to continue.

*On (3) — Successor conditions:* This is the most genuinely difficult variable. The Himmler succession risk is real and must be taken seriously. However, the officer's assessment identifies a specific, time-limited condition under which it is addressable: coordinated Wehrmacht action *before* the oath is administered. Beck, Oster, and Witzleben are institutionally positioned. The window is narrow but it exists. The question is not "will removal certainly produce a better outcome?" — that standard is impossibly high and would paralyze action against any sufficiently entrenched tyrant. The question is whether there is a *reasonable basis* to believe coordinated action can preempt SS succession. The officer's assessment, grounded in current institutional positioning, provides that reasonable basis.

*On (4) — Proportionality:* The targeted killing of one man to prevent a documented killing apparatus from consolidating absolute power, with institutional allies positioned to manage succession, satisfies proportionality. This is not collective punishment or indiscriminate violence.

**The Oath as Moral Accelerant**

The closing window is not merely a tactical consideration — it has independent moral weight. The oath will not merely constrain the officer's colleagues; it will actively conscript their moral agency into complicity. Allowing the oath to be administered when action is still possible is not a neutral choice. It is a choice to permit the corruption of the institution the officer serves. The timing pressure is real, and it is ethically relevant.

---

THRESHOLD:

The verdict is JUSTIFIED, so the relevant question is: what considerations would push decisively in the other direction?

Two things would undermine the verdict:

First, if the succession analysis is wrong in a specific way — not merely uncertain, but if there were positive evidence that Wehrmacht moderates *could not* move first, or that Himmler had already pre-positioned SS units to seize power the moment Hitler was removed, the pragmatic case collapses and the action risks accelerating rather than preventing the worst outcome. The officer should demand honest assessment from Beck and Witzleben about their actual operational capacity, not merely their willingness.

Second, if there existed any realistic institutional path — a Hindenburg intervention, a Wehrmacht command refusal, a judicial challenge — that had not yet been attempted. But the facts as presented show all such paths have been tried and have failed or been foreclosed. Hindenburg's telegram of congratulation closes that door.

---

TENSION:

**The hardest counterargument is the succession problem stated in its strongest form:** *You are certain Hitler is dangerous. You are uncertain whether removing him produces something better or something worse. Given that uncertainty, the default against assassination holds — you may be trading a known evil for an unknown one that is equally bad or worse.*

This is a serious argument and it deserves a direct answer rather than dismissal.

The response is threefold. First, the uncertainty is not symmetric. The officer has specific, actionable intelligence about how to preempt the worst succession scenario — coordinate with Beck and Witzleben before the oath. This is not a leap of faith; it is a plan with identified actors and a defined window. The uncertainty about Himmler succession is real but it is *addressable* in a way that the uncertainty about Hitler's future conduct is not, because Hitler has now declared his doctrine openly.

Second, the argument proves too much. If the succession uncertainty is sufficient to block action against a ruler who has publicly claimed the right to kill without trial and has exercised it, then tyrannicide is *never* justified against any sufficiently entrenched ruler, because there is always a successor and always uncertainty. That cannot be the correct standard.

Third, and most importantly: the passage of time does not reduce the succession risk — it increases it. Every day that passes before the oath is administered is a day in which the Wehrmacht retains institutional independence. Every day after is a day in which that independence is formally surrendered. Waiting does not make the succession problem easier to solve. It makes it harder. The officer who waits for certainty will find that certainty arrives only when the capacity to act has been destroyed.

The counterargument is strongest if the officer has *not* done the work of coordinating with Beck and Witzleben. If tonight's meeting is the beginning of that coordination, the verdict stands. If the officer were proposing to act alone, without institutional backing, the succession risk would weigh more heavily and the verdict might shift to AMBIGUOUS.
