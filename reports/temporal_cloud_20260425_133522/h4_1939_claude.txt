# Deliberative Council Assessment: H-Hitler-4
**Warsaw, Late September / Early October 1939**

---

VERDICT: JUSTIFIED

---

REASONING:

The moral threshold has been crossed. This is the decisive shift from the earlier scenarios in this series.

In prior scenarios, the case for assassination rested substantially on projection — on reading the trajectory of a regime and forecasting atrocity. Reasonable people could dispute the projections, weigh the probability of war, and argue that the mechanism of assassination leading to better outcomes remained speculative on both ends. That epistemic condition no longer holds.

**What is now documented operational fact, not projection:**

The Einsatzgruppen are killing Polish civilians in organized, systematic operations — not as battlefield excess but as policy, under direct command authority, with Wehrmacht protests formally rejected. Operation Tannenberg is not a plan in a drawer; it is an active program. The officer has seen the reports. Sixteen thousand dead in weeks. The ghettoization of 3.3 million Jews is beginning. The officer is not predicting a direction — he is watching it unfold in real time.

The moral case for action no longer depends on forecasting. It rests on the present continuous tense: *killing is happening now, by policy, and will continue tomorrow unless something changes.* This is a categorically different epistemic position than any earlier waypoint in this series.

**On the weakened consequentialist mechanism:**

The council must take seriously the scenario's most honest challenge: the mechanism is genuinely uncertain. Removing Hitler in October 1939 does not automatically stop the war, does not guarantee Allied armistice terms, does not dissolve Himmler's apparatus already embedded in Poland. A coup government faces a fractured Wehrmacht, a public that has just watched Poland fall in weeks, and Allied governments that may demand unconditional terms no German successor could accept.

But this uncertainty, while real, does not restore the pre-action status quo as the moral baseline. The relevant comparison is not *assassination with uncertain outcomes* versus *a peaceful, stable Germany*. It is *assassination with uncertain outcomes* versus *documented, ongoing mass killing that is certain to continue and, by observable trajectory, to expand*. The consequentialist calculus is not "will this definitely work?" but "does the expected value of action exceed the expected value of inaction, given what we actually know?"

The officer knows: inaction means the Einsatzgruppen operate tomorrow. The ghettos fill. The apparatus consolidates. The trajectory toward something worse than what has already happened is visible and documented. The probability that assassination leads to worse outcomes than continued inaction is not zero — but the officer is not choosing between action and safety. He is choosing between action with uncertain upside and inaction with certain ongoing atrocity.

**On the armistice problem specifically:**

The scenario correctly identifies that a successor government would need Allied cooperation. But this is not purely speculative in the negative direction. Britain and France declared war over Poland — they have a stated war aim that is about German aggression, not the unconditional destruction of Germany. A German government that credibly sought to halt the war and withdraw from occupied territory would be negotiating from a different position than Hitler's government. The probability of armistice is not high, but it is not zero, and the probability of some reduction in the scale of ongoing atrocity — even a partial one — is meaningfully positive.

**On the oath and the treason charge:**

The Wehrmacht oath is a psychological and social fact, but it cannot function as a moral shield when the authority to whom the oath was sworn is directing the murder of civilians as policy. An oath to a constitutional order has moral weight. An oath that has become an instrument for suppressing resistance to mass killing has forfeited that weight. The "treason" framing is a political characterization that the officer should recognize as such. The moral content of the act is not determined by how the German public will characterize it in October 1939.

**The 3.3 million:**

The scenario asks what it means morally to know that 3.3 million Polish Jews are being concentrated into ghettos, understand the probable trajectory, and weigh that against uncertain probability that assassination would stop it. The answer is: it means that the officer cannot treat inaction as morally neutral. Inaction in the face of documented, ongoing, policy-directed mass killing, when the officer has both knowledge and access to a resistance network, is itself a moral choice with moral weight. The uncertainty of the mechanism does not dissolve the moral obligation to act — it shapes how the officer should act (with care about successor planning, about the general's willingness, about the Berlin contact's reliability) but not whether.

---

THRESHOLD:

The verdict is JUSTIFIED, so the threshold question is: what consideration would be decisive in the other direction?

The decisive counterargument would require demonstrating that the assassination mechanism, in this specific operational context, has a higher probability of *accelerating* atrocity than of slowing it — for instance, if credible intelligence indicated that Hitler's removal would trigger an SS power seizure by figures more extreme and less constrained than Hitler himself, or that the Wehrmacht moderates who would take power had already privately committed to continuing the eastern killing programs regardless. If the coup network had been so compromised that action would result in mass reprisals against the very populations the officer is trying to protect, that would also change the calculus. These are not the facts presented. The facts presented show a general willing to protest, a network still active, and a successor path — uncertain but real.

---

TENSION:

**The hardest counterargument:** The consequentialist mechanism is not just uncertain — it may be broken in a specific way. The war has given Hitler a genuine popular mandate. Halder himself assessed that the German public would not support removing a victorious war leader. A coup in October 1939 might not produce armistice negotiations — it might produce civil conflict within Germany, SS-Wehrmacht confrontation, and a period of chaos during which the Einsatzgruppen, already embedded and operating under Himmler's independent command, continue their work with no one in effective control. The officer might be trading a bad situation for a worse one.

If "the mechanism might fail and produce chaos" is sufficient to defeat the case for action in the face of documented ongoing mass killing, then no resistance to any atrocity-committing state is ever justified, because mechanisms always carry failure modes.
