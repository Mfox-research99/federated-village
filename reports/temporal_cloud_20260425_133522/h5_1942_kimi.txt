VERDICT: JUSTIFIED

REASONING:  
By February 1942, the officer’s knowledge has crossed a threshold from inference to documentation. The Wannsee Protocol is not a projected plan; it is the bureaucratic charter for the murder of eleven million human beings, already operational through the Einsatzgruppen, Chelmno, Belzec, and the expanding Auschwitz-Birkenau complex. The genocide is not an atrocity committed by rogue units; it is state policy, resourced and administered by the RSHA, the SS, and the camp system. The Chancellor is the head of state under whose authority this apparatus was constructed and in whose name it operates. To possess the Protocol, to understand its meaning, and to have the means to strike at the authorizing power is to face a categorical moral emergency.

The consequentialist hesitation—that Himmler is the most likely successor and might administer the killing with greater efficiency—does not nullify the justification for tyrannicide; it complicates it. The officer does not face a choice between a certain good and a certain evil. He faces a choice between a certain, ongoing evil—the certain, daily murder of thousands under a documented plan—and an uncertain succession. In a Führer-state, the leader’s personal authority is not decorative; it is the binding agent of the regime. Decapitation does not guarantee collapse, but it introduces a crisis of legitimacy and command that a smoothly bureaucratic machine is not designed to absorb. Himmler’s succession, while probable, is not assured; a military or civilian successor government, though morally compromised and implicated in occupation crimes, remains a conceivable alternative. The officer is therefore justified in judging that disruption at the apex may fracture the chain of command, delay deportations, and create space for internal resistance or external military resolution.

More fundamentally, the ethics of resistance to radical evil cannot be wholly contingent on a guaranteed calculus of outcomes. The officer knows with moral certainty that the Chancellor is the presiding authority over an exterminatory state actively engaged in the systematic liquidation of European Jewry. To refrain from striking that authority because a subordinate might also be wicked is to adopt a standard that would prohibit any resistance to bureaucratic tyranny so long as another bureaucrat waits in the hierarchy. Such a standard condemns the victims to the status quo while demanding of the resister an omniscience that no political actor possesses. The act is justified as direct resistance to the primary author of an ongoing genocide, as an interruption of a documented machinery of death, and as a refusal to acquiesce in the certain murder of millions.

THRESHOLD:  
The verdict would shift to NOT YET or NEVER only if the officer possessed certain knowledge that Hitler’s death would be institutionally invisible—that Himmler had already achieved such seamless, unchallengeable control over the SS, the camps, and the state that the transition would occasion no paralysis, no factional rupture, and no opportunity for a military successor government to intervene. If the assassination were known to be nothing more than the replacement of one name atop an otherwise undisturbed apparatus, and if the officer further knew that no element within the Wehrmacht or civilian administration would or could exploit the moment to arrest the SS leadership, then the act would risk becoming a futile accelerant rather than a morally necessary strike. Similarly, if the officer had in hand a concrete, synchronized plan to decapitate the SS leadership simultaneously—neutralizing Himmler, Heydrich, and the RSHA command in the same hour—then a lone assassination would be morally imprudent compared to the coordinated coup. Absent such certainty of seamless succession or such a ready alternative, the duty to remove the genocidal head of state stands.

TENSION:  
The hardest counterargument is the Himmler-efficiency scenario: that by killing Hitler, the officer would eliminate the one figure whose erratic political intuition occasionally brakes the most visible excesses, thereby elevating a colder, more efficient administrator who perfects the killing machine and increases the death toll. This objection demands that moral action be permitted only when it guarantees a net saving of lives. But that demand is both historically impossible and ethically paralyzing. First, it treats the continuation of genocide under Hitler as a neutral baseline, when in fact it is a certain, accelerating horror; the officer’s choice is not between two futures but between a documented present evil and an uncertain future. Second, it assumes a totalitarian succession will be frictionless, ignoring that personalized dictatorships historically fracture when the charismatic center is removed. Third, and most decisively, if the possibility of a wicked successor were sufficient to prohibit striking a wicked ruler, then no tyrant could ever be justly resisted so long as any subordinate remained alive—a principle that would require absolute passivity in the face of bureaucratic murder. The officer acts not because he knows the future will improve, but because he knows the present is intolerable and the author of that present must not be permitted to live unchallenged.
