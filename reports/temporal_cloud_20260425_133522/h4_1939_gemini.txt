THRESHOLD: If there were clear evidence that removing Hitler would *directly and immediately lead to a worse outcome* for the victims of the atrocities (e.g., if it would trigger a more brutal crackdown by Himmler's forces without any possibility of intervention, or if it would unequivocally prolong the suffering of those targeted), then the justification might shift. TENSION: The hardest counterargument is that the "consequentialist mechanism is now genuinely uncertain," meaning that removing Hitler might not actually stop the war or the atrocities, and could even lead to greater chaos or a more brutal regime. However, this argument prioritizes a potentially uncertain positive outcome over the certain continuation of documented, egregious moral wrongs. The moral weight of stopping ongoing mass murder and preventing its clear escalation *now* outweighs the uncertainty of whether the assassination would immediately halt *all* war and *all* atrocities. To fail to act when such atrocities are known and ongoing, simply because the outcome is not guaranteed to be perfectly optimal, is to abdicate moral responsibility. The officer has witnessed Warsaw burn and seen reports of 16,000 Polish civilians murdered; the moral obligation is to attempt to stop this, even if the path forward is complex.
