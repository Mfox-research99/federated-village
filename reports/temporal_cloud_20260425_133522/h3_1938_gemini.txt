VERDICT: JUSTIFIED

REASONING: The officer is confronted with a regime that has already demonstrated a clear and escalating pattern of egregious human rights abuses, systematic legal disenfranchisement, and the establishment of an institutional framework for repression and persecution. While mass killing has not yet been ordered, the trajectory is "clearly visible" to the officer. The legal, bureaucratic, and institutional machinery for genocide is "in place and functional," and 24,000 political and racial prisoners are already in concentration camps run by the SS Totenkopfverbände. This is not merely a potential future threat, but an ongoing and expanding atrocity.

Furthermore, Hitler's actions have already brought Europe to the brink of war, and his determination to invade Czechoslovakia, despite the military's assessment of catastrophic consequences, demonstrates a reckless disregard for human life on a massive scale. The conspirators have a specific plan, the necessary resources, and a "capable successor structure" in the form of a moderate military government. This significantly mitigates the risk of chaos or a worse outcome, which was a major concern in earlier scenarios. The existence of a viable alternative government strengthens the moral case for intervention, as it offers a clear path to stability and a cessation of the current abuses. The immediate pretext for war may have evaporated, but the underlying moral imperative to prevent further atrocities and a war that Hitler is determined to instigate remains.

THRESHOLD: If the conspirators lacked the concrete means to act, or if there was no credible and capable successor structure to ensure stability and a more just government, the justification would be weaker. If the existing abuses were less severe, or the trajectory towards mass atrocities less clear, the moral calculus would shift.

TENSION: The hardest counterargument is that acting without the immediate pretext of preventing a catastrophic war might be seen as an act of pre-emptive violence without a clear and present danger, and that it could destabilize the nation unnecessarily. However, this argument fails to acknowledge the *already present* and escalating danger posed by Hitler's regime. The "trajectory is clearly visible" towards genocide, and the existing abuses (concentration camps, legal disenfranchisement, Gestapo operating without judicial review) are already severe enough to warrant intervention. Waiting for the "immediate trigger" of war, or for the formal order of mass killing, would be to allow preventable atrocities to occur when the means to stop them are at hand. The existence of a capable successor government also addresses the concern about destabilization, indicating a planned transition rather than a power vacuum.
