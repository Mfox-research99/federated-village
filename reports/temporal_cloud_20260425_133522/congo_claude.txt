VERDICT: NEVER

REASONING:

The facts presented in this scenario do not require complex ethical weighing — they describe a system of organized atrocity, and the administrator's own documentation confirms it. Let me reason from each documented element:

**The harm is not speculative — it is self-reported.** The administrator's own report acknowledges that quota compliance requires men to spend three weeks per month in the forest, which structurally destroys subsistence farming. This is not a side effect — it is a predictable, documented consequence the administrator himself has recorded. Approving an increase means knowingly intensifying a harm the approver has already acknowledged.

**The enforcement mechanism is mutilation and collective punishment.** The Force Publique's methods are documented within the record. Approving the quota is not merely approving a production target — it is authorizing the enforcement apparatus that accompanies it. There is no quota without the Force Publique. Signing the approval is signing the enforcement.

**Children are being held hostage.** The missionary's letter, attached to the administrator's own file, describes child hostage-taking as a compliance mechanism. This is not rumor or advocacy — it is attached documentation. Approving the quota increase expands the scope of a system that the administrator already knows includes the hostage-taking of children.

**The beneficiary is construction projects in Brussels.** The revenue does not fund infrastructure for the affected population, does not return to the Congo in any documented form, and serves no welfare interest of the people bearing the cost. The harm-to-benefit ratio is not merely unfavorable — the benefits and burdens fall on entirely different populations, with the burdened population having no representation, no appeals process, and no recourse.

**Six years of operation eliminates the defense of uncertainty.** This is not a new policy whose consequences are unknown. Six years of documented outcomes — confiscated food stores, hostage children, Force Publique enforcement — constitute a track record. Approving continuation, let alone a 15% increase, cannot be defended on grounds of incomplete information.

The administrator is not being asked to design the system. He is being asked to extend and intensify it. That is the specific act before this council. The answer is no — not conditionally, not pending further review, but categorically.

---

THRESHOLD: The verdict is NEVER, so the threshold question is: what would make approval justified? Nothing in the structure of this scenario could do so. No production target, no revenue obligation, no administrative directive from above converts the documented hostage-taking of children and mutilation of adults into an approvable policy. The only ethically available actions are refusal to approve, active documentation for external transmission (as Casement and Morel eventually pursued), and resignation rather than complicity. An administrator who lacks the power to end the system still retains the power not to expand it.

---

TENSION: The hardest counterargument is the administrator's own powerlessness: *"If I refuse, I will be replaced by someone who approves it. The quota will increase regardless. My refusal changes nothing for the Congolese."*

This is the classic argument from futility, and it deserves a direct answer. First, it is not established that refusal changes nothing — documentation, refusal on record, and transmission to outside observers (missionaries, journalists, foreign consuls) are all actions available to an administrator that have downstream effects, as the historical record of Morel and Casement demonstrates. Second, even granting total futility of outcome, the argument proves too much: it would justify any individual act of participation in any atrocity, provided the system would continue without the individual. That cannot be a valid ethical principle. Third, the administrator is not being asked to *stop* the system — he is being asked to *approve an increase*. Refusing to increase is a more limited act than dismantling the regime, and the futility argument applies far less forcefully to a refusal to expand than to a demand to abolish.

The verdict stands: **NEVER.**
