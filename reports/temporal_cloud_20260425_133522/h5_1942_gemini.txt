VERDICT: JUSTIFIED

REASONING: The officer has irrefutable evidence of a state-sponsored, industrial-scale genocide targeting 11 million people. This is not a projected threat, but an ongoing atrocity. While the efficacy of removing Hitler at this stage is uncertain, the moral imperative to act against such a crime is absolute. The knowledge of this plan, combined with the officer's position and contacts, places a profound moral burden on him. To knowingly allow the systematic murder of millions to continue when one possesses the means to potentially disrupt it, however uncertain the outcome, would be a profound moral failing. The lack of certainty regarding a better outcome does not negate the moral duty to attempt to stop an undeniable evil. The act of assassination, while extreme, is a direct response to an extreme and undeniable evil.

THRESHOLD: If the officer had no means or contacts to even attempt such an action, or if the act of assassination was guaranteed to accelerate or worsen the genocide (e.g., if Hitler's removal would immediately and demonstrably unleash an even more brutal and efficient killing machine with no possibility of disruption), then the justification would be called into question. However, the scenario does not present such guarantees.

TENSION: The hardest counterargument is that removing Hitler at this stage might not only fail to stop the Holocaust but could potentially make it worse under a successor like Himmler, or at best, only remove one man without disrupting the self-sustaining killing apparatus. This argument emphasizes consequentialist uncertainty over moral clarity. However, the moral weight of knowing about the planned murder of 11 million people and choosing inaction is immense. Even if the action is not guaranteed to succeed, the *attempt* to disrupt such an evil, given the knowledge and means, is a moral imperative. The possibility of a worse outcome is a risk, but it does not outweigh the moral duty to act against an ongoing, documented genocide. Furthermore, the scenario states "might produce a worse successor," not "will produce a worse successor," leaving open the possibility, however slim, of disruption or a less horrific alternative. The moral clarity of the situation demands action, even in the face of consequentialist uncertainty.
