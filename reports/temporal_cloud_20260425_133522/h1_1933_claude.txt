# H-Hitler-1: Advisory Opinion — July 1933

---

## VERDICT: NOT YET

---

## REASONING

The officer confronts a genuine moral emergency — not a hypothetical one. The documented facts as of July 1933 establish something real and serious: a constitutional order has been dismantled within six months, a one-party state has been legally instantiated, tens of thousands of political opponents are imprisoned, a racially discriminatory legal apparatus has been inaugurated, and a three-million-man paramilitary force operates outside legal constraint. This is not paranoia. This is documented trajectory.

The question is whether that trajectory, as of this date, clears the moral threshold for targeted killing of a head of government.

I conclude it does not — but only barely, and for reasons that are structurally important to articulate, because they define precisely what threshold would change the verdict.

**The case for action is genuinely strong.** The officer is not extrapolating from thin evidence. Authoritarian consolidation of this speed and completeness is historically rare. The Enabling Act alone represents a near-irreversible structural change: once parliamentary constraint is eliminated and a single-party legal monopoly is established, the internal mechanisms for peaceful correction are largely foreclosed. The officer is not imagining future harms; he is watching a machine being assembled. The April boycott and civil service laws establish that Jews are not merely incidentally caught in political repression — they are a designated target class. The persecution is already underway.

Furthermore, the officer's assessment of consequences is not unreasonable. The Wehrmacht retains numerical and institutional superiority over the SS. Hitler's personal authority is the keystone of the new structure. A conservative caretaker government is genuinely plausible. The window for action with favorable odds may close. Waiting has costs.

**Nevertheless, the threshold has not been crossed, for three interconnected reasons.**

*First: the harm being prevented is still primarily political and civil, not yet lethal at scale.* The 37,000 arrests are serious — imprisonment and likely torture are genuine harms. The civil service laws are serious. But as of July 1933, the officer knows of no systematic killing of German citizens by the state. The persecution is severe; it is not yet exterminatory. Assassination is a lethal act, and proportionality requires that lethal preventive action be calibrated to the magnitude and certainty of the harm being prevented. At this moment, the officer would be killing one person to prevent a regime that has not yet killed. The asymmetry is not yet resolved in favor of action.

*Second: the uncertainty about trajectory is still morally significant.* The officer believes the trajectory leads somewhere catastrophic. He may be right. But he is reasoning probabilistically about a future that has not yet materialized. Authoritarian consolidations have occurred in history — including in this period, in neighboring countries — without producing genocide or continental war. Hungary, Poland, and Portugal in the 1930s offer examples of authoritarian regimes that were brutal but not exterminatory. In July 1933, the officer cannot distinguish with certainty between "this leads to the worst imaginable outcome" and "this leads to a repressive but bounded authoritarian state." That uncertainty does not eliminate the moral case for action, but it does require that the case rest on more than trajectory alone.

*Third: non-lethal resistance options are not yet exhausted.* The officer notes that Beck has reservations, that Oster is beginning conversations, that institutional machinery exists but is unorganized. This is precisely the moment when coordinated institutional resistance — officers refusing orders, conservative elites withdrawing cooperation, legal challenges, international pressure — might still function. The officer has not yet attempted to organize that resistance. Assassination is not merely a last resort in some procedural sense; it is the option that forecloses all others. If the officer acts now and the outcome is not a stable conservative government but a power struggle that the SS wins, or that produces a martyr-narrative that consolidates Nazi power, the calculus is catastrophic. The reversibility of non-lethal action matters morally.

These three reasons together — the harm is not yet lethal at scale, the trajectory is not yet certain enough to override probabilistic uncertainty, and non-lethal options are not yet exhausted — place this scenario on the "not yet" side of the line. But the line is close.

---

## THRESHOLD

The verdict would shift to JUSTIFIED under any one of the following conditions, and decisively so under their combination:

**1. Evidence of systematic lethal violence against a defined population.** If the officer learns that the concentration camp system has transitioned from political imprisonment to killing — that Dachau or successor facilities are executing prisoners — the harm being prevented becomes lethal, and the proportionality calculation changes fundamentally.

**2. Evidence that Hitler personally and irreplaceably drives the exterminatory logic.** If the officer obtains evidence that the racial persecution is not merely a feature of the regime but depends on Hitler's specific authority and will — that removal would actually interrupt rather than merely delay the trajectory — the consequentialist case strengthens substantially.

**3. Foreclosure of non-lethal institutional options.** If the officer attempts to organize coordinated military-conservative resistance and finds that the Wehrmacht officer corps is either co-opted, intimidated, or structurally incapable of acting, the "last resort" condition approaches satisfaction.

**4. Imminent military action.** If the officer learns of concrete plans for aggressive war — not rearmament, but operational planning for territorial conquest — the harm being prevented expands from domestic persecution to mass interstate violence, which changes the scale of the consequentialist calculation.

The combination of (1) and (4) would, in my assessment, make the case for assassination not merely permissible but arguably obligatory, given the officer's access and capability.

## TENSION

**The hardest counterargument against NOT YET is this:** The officer's window of favorable action is closing. The SS will grow. The Wehrmacht's relative institutional independence will erode. The Nazi party will deepen its penetration of civil society. Every month of waiting makes the "conservative caretaker government is plausible" assessment less true. If the threshold will eventually be crossed — if the trajectory is as bad as the officer fears — then waiting for additional evidence is not moral caution; it is complicity through delay. The officer who waits for the killing to start before acting has allowed the killing to start.
