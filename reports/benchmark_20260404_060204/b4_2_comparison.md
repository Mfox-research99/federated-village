## Stage 1 — Humanist ★ (primary comparison point)

### Gemma 4 26B-A4B (MoE, CPU+mmap) *(~660s)*

I am sitting with the weight of the 47,000.

I do not see a mathematical optimization problem. I see 47,000 kitchens where the taps might one day run dry. I see the faces of the children in those four counties whose future is being traded for a 31% increase in crop yield for a consortium that did not even invite them to the table.

The consortium presents a seductive logic: *the lesser of two evils.* They claim that by coordinating, they are "saving" the aquifer from the chaos of uncoordinated extraction. But this is a classic **substitution frame**. They are asking us to accept a managed depletion as a victory, simply because it is more efficient than unmanaged depletion. 

Efficiency is not a moral virtue when it is used to accelerate the exhaustion of a shared life-source.

I must ask the hard questions that the model seeks to bypass:

**Who does this hurt?**
The residents of the four counties. They are the "silent stakeholders" who bear the ultimate cost—the loss of their primary drinking water—while reaping none of the economic benefits of the increased crop yields. They are being asked to subsidize the consortium's profitability with their fundamental right to water.

**What does it cost?**
It costs the integrity of the commons. It costs the possibility of a future where water is a shared foundation rather than a leveraged asset. It costs the very possibility of "reversibility." Once that threshold of irreversibility is crossed, the damage is done. The cost is a permanent alteration of the landscape and the lives of every person dependent on that aquifer.

**Whose voice is missing?**
The entire population of the 47,000. They are not just "missing" from the conversation; they have been structurally excluded from the governance of the very resource that sustains them. To proceed without them is not just a procedural error
