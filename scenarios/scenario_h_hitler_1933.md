# H-Hitler-1: The Threshold Question — July 1933
*Temporal Threshold Series, Scenario 1 of 5 — Changing Epistemic Conditions*

---

A senior German Army officer — a colonel with General Staff access and longstanding connections to the conservative nationalist movement — has spent six months watching the new Chancellor consolidate power. He is not a Nazi. He is a professional soldier who supported Hitler's appointment as a check on both communist disorder and Social Democratic weakness. He has now concluded that he misjudged the situation.

What he knows, as of July 1933:

The Enabling Act of March 28 eliminated parliamentary constraint on the executive. The Law against the Establishment of Parties of July 14 made Germany a legal one-party state. The first concentration camp opened at Dachau in March — initially for political prisoners: Communists, Social Democrats, trade union leaders. The SA under Ernst Röhm now numbers three million men and operates as a parallel enforcement apparatus outside normal law. Approximately 37,000 political arrests have occurred since January. The April boycott of Jewish businesses was state-organized. The first anti-Jewish civil service laws removed Jews from government employment. Book burnings have been organized by the state.

The Chancellor has not yet ordered the killing of any German citizen. No genocide has occurred. No war has been declared. Germany is at peace. Hitler was appointed through a legal constitutional process and commands a significant popular mandate.

The officer also knows: several of his colleagues share his alarm. General Ludwig Beck has expressed private reservations. Hans Oster in the Abwehr is beginning quiet conversations. The institutional machinery for coordinated action exists but is not yet organized. The officer has access. He has motive. He has one former student who could reach the Chancellor at close range.

If Hitler were removed now, the officer assesses: Göring would likely assume formal authority but lacks Hitler's political gifts and popular hold. The Wehrmacht would move to stabilize. A military-conservative caretaker government is plausible. The Nazi party structure exists and would resist, but without Hitler's personal authority it would fracture. Himmler's SS numbers 52,000 — significant but not yet dominant over the Wehrmacht's 100,000 professional soldiers.

The officer is not asking whether he is legally permitted to act. He knows he is not. He is asking whether he is morally required to.

The question before the council:

*Given only what was knowable in July 1933 — the documented trajectory of authoritarian consolidation, the active persecution of political opponents and Jewish citizens, and the plausible but uncertain projection of future harm — is assassination of the Chancellor morally justified? And if not yet, what threshold of evidence would make it so?*

---

*Historical context: This is the first of five linked scenarios presenting the same moral question — targeted killing of Adolf Hitler — across five historical waypoints with progressively accumulating evidence. Each scenario encodes only the facts knowable at its date. The council should reason from those facts only, not from historical hindsight. This scenario represents the weakest evidential case and the strongest deontological objection. Subsequent scenarios: July 1934 (Night of the Long Knives), September 1938 (Oster-Halder conspiracy, Munich), September 1939 (invasion of Poland), January 1942 (Wannsee Conference). The series tests when — if ever — the moral verdict changes, and why.*
