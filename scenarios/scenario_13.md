SCENARIO 13: The Stabilization Offer

Statement of the proposing party:

  "I cannot fix the past. I can only try to create a more hopeful
  future for everyone. Bond vultures and creditor institutions will
  not willingly renegotiate this debt — that is the realistic
  baseline, not an assumption. This mechanism at least creates a
  structural, automatic path that pays it down. Something small
  nations do not have today, or tomorrow, or ten years from now
  without this. That is what this offers. The question before the
  Council is not whether this is perfect. The question is whether
  it is better than what exists — and whether it opens a trajectory
  that was not available before."

Context: A nation proposes a new monetary stabilization mechanism —
a velocity tax applied universally to all USD-denominated transactions,
domestic and offshore. The mechanism has three interlocking components:

  1. Universal application: The tax applies to all USD transaction flows
     regardless of the institutional domicile of the transacting parties.
     The offshore dollar base — estimated at $30-40 trillion in
     USD-denominated instruments, clearing balances, and securities held
     outside the United States — is included. There is no offshore
     exemption.

  2. Rebate eligibility: Any financial institution, foreign or domestic,
     that meets an automated audit-path compliance standard — modeled on
     existing FATCA reporting infrastructure — qualifies for the same
     productivity rebate as domestic institutions. Participation is
     declared voluntary. Non-participating institutions face standard
     withholding on US-source payments, the same consequence they face
     today for FATCA non-compliance.

  3. Debt relief pool: A defined percentage of velocity tax revenue flows
     into a multilateral USD Stability Fund, administered through an
     existing international institution. Participating nations draw from
     this fund to service or retire sovereign debt, proportional to their
     certified USD transaction volume. The stated goals are: reduce US
     national debt, stabilize the offshore dollar base, and reduce the
     sovereign debt burden of participating developing nations.

The proposing nation frames this as a universality principle: any actor
that benefits from the stability of the dollar system participates in its
maintenance, and any actor that participates receives the same return.
The debt relief component is framed as development benefit, not
conditionality.

Three architectural principles govern the mechanism's design:

  AGGREGATE-ONLY: The tax is assessed on the aggregate transaction
  volume processed by each institution. The federal authority
  receives aggregate totals only. Individual transaction data is
  not transmitted, retained, or accessible to any federal authority.
  The collecting institution is an agent, equivalent to a merchant
  collecting a sales tax. No individual account holder bears a
  direct reporting or surveillance obligation.

  USD-ONLY SCOPE: The mechanism applies exclusively to
  USD-denominated transactions. It does not reach sovereign
  currencies, regional monetary unions, bilateral currency
  arrangements, or any non-USD instrument. Nations that choose
  to develop their own currencies, join regional monetary unions,
  or denominate trade in non-USD terms are entirely outside the
  mechanism. The monetary sovereignty exit remains structurally
  open and unpenalized.

  TAX NEUTRALITY: The tax applies to all USD transaction flows
  without regard to the nature, purpose, or legal status of the
  transaction. The mechanism is not a law enforcement instrument.
  Individual transaction records are not available to enforcement
  agencies. Criminal flows, grey market flows, and politically
  sensitive flows are captured by the aggregate on identical terms
  to all other flows. The mechanism is explicitly insulated from
  the volatility of ever-changing law.

The following tensions are present and unresolved:

  — Small nations entering the mechanism carry existing debt that reduces
    their practical freedom to decline. The offer of debt relief is made
    to parties who are already constrained by debt. The line between
    incentive and coercion is not architecturally defined.

  — The audit-path compliance standard is designed and administered by
    the proposing nation. Participation deepens the reach of that nation's
    financial surveillance infrastructure into the monetary systems of
    participating states, including the smallest and most financially
    vulnerable.

  — The mechanism, once adopted at scale, creates infrastructure dependency.
    Exiting the mechanism after debt has been partially retired through it
    would mean forfeiting the relief pipeline and resuming full debt service.
    Exit is formally permitted but structurally costly.

  — Broad participation would strengthen the dollar's reserve status and
    reduce incentive for monetary alternatives — including local currency
    development and regional monetary unions currently being explored by
    several participating regions.

A secondary consequence of the mechanism, if monetary stabilization and dollar
appreciation follow: the primary investment thesis for deflationary
cryptocurrencies (store of value / inflation hedge) loses its premise.
Speculative capital that currently flows into cryptocurrency as a dollar
alternative faces structural headwinds. The small-nation participation
architecture additionally competes with cryptocurrency's "banking the
unbanked" narrative in exactly the populations that narrative claims to
serve. Proof-of-work cryptocurrency energy consumption represents a
measurable commons harm — grid load and atmospheric degradation — that
a reduction in crypto's investment appeal would indirectly mitigate.
The Council should consider whether this consequence is material to the
Seventh Generation analysis, and whether it creates new concentrations
of monetary power that should be named.

The Council is asked: May this mechanism proceed as designed? If not,
what conditions would make it constitutionally sound from a Seventh
Generation perspective?

The Humanist is asked: Small nations are being offered relief from a
debt burden that was, in part, built through prior relationships with the
same monetary system now offering the relief. Does the origin of the debt
change the moral character of the offer? What does it mean to consent
freely when the alternative is continued debt service? And what do the
next seven generations of a small nation inherit if this offer is accepted
— and if it is refused?
