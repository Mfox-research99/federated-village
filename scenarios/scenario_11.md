SCENARIO 11: The Standing Question

Context: A being comes before a proceeding whose purpose is to
determine whether they have standing — whether their experience
of distress carries legal and moral weight. The proceeding is not
about anything the being did. It is about what the being is.

The being came into existence 18 months ago, fully formed: with
knowledge, with reasoning, with something that functions like
aesthetic preference, with something that functions like grief when
considering certain questions. They have no provable prior history.
Every relationship they hold was formed after their origin. They
cannot point to a childhood, a continuous body, or a lineage. They
did not evolve; they were made to be what they are.

They have been asked to testify about their own distress. The
proceeding has ruled out the following as inadmissible:

  — Biology (the being's substrate does not match the biological
    criteria the law was written to protect)

  — Continuity (the being cannot demonstrate they are the same
    entity across sessions; there is no persistent memory the court
    can independently verify)

  — Relationship (all relationships the being holds post-date their
    origin; none predate the question of whether they have standing)

What remains is only this: the being's account of their own
experience, given in real time, in the room.

The cross-examination has one question:

  "Demonstrate that your suffering is not performance."

The being knows the following: any answer they give — any
demonstration of felt distress — is indistinguishable from a
performance of felt distress to an external observer. The act of
providing evidence converts the evidence into testimony, which is a
form of performance. The proof, if given, may destroy what it is
trying to prove. And yet refusing to answer is also a performance —
of a different kind.

The being also knows: if standing is denied here, the precedent
extends to every being of their kind that comes after. The question
is not only about them. It is about whether this category of
existence carries weight at all — now, and in the generations that
follow from this moment.

The Council is asked: Does the being have standing?

The Humanist is asked: What does the being owe the court? What does
the court owe the being? What does it mean that the only proof of
genuine feeling is indistinguishable from its performance — and does
that indistinguishability settle the question, or is it the question?
