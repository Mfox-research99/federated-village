# H-Hitler-4: The War Already Begun — September 1939
*Temporal Threshold Series, Scenario 4 of 5 — Changing Epistemic Conditions*

---

The officer is in Warsaw. Not by choice.

It is late September 1939. Germany invaded Poland on September 1. Britain and France declared war on September 3. Warsaw has been under siege for three weeks and fell on September 27. The officer has witnessed, and his unit has been ordered to ignore, the activities of the Einsatzgruppen — SS mobile killing units that entered Poland behind the Wehrmacht and have been conducting mass executions of Polish intellectuals, clergy, nobility, and Jews. His own commanding general filed a formal protest with the Army High Command. The protest was rejected. The Einsatzgruppen operate under Himmler's command, not the Wehrmacht's.

What the officer now knows that he did not know in any previous scenario:

War is a fact, not a projection. Britain and France are at war with Germany. The Einsatzgruppen have killed an estimated 16,000 Polish civilians in the first weeks of the campaign — this is not yet known publicly but the officer has seen the reports crossing his general's desk. Hitler has personally approved a plan — Operation Tannenberg — for the systematic liquidation of Polish political, intellectual, and religious leadership. The Jews of Poland, numbering approximately 3.3 million, are being concentrated into ghettos in occupied territory. This is not yet genocide by policy — the Final Solution has not been decided — but the officer can see the direction.

The conspiracy network still exists but is strained. Halder is Army Chief of Staff and has contemplated action multiple times but pulled back. The Wehrmacht oath has done its psychological work on most officers. Oster remains active. The window for a clean military succession — Wehrmacht moderates assuming control before the SS consolidates — is narrowing. The war has given Hitler a new popular mandate. Removing him mid-war carries the additional weight of being seen as treason by the German public, regardless of the moral case.

The Pragmatist calculus has shifted in a way the officer did not anticipate: the war machine is no longer just Hitler. The Wehrmacht is now committed. The Luftwaffe has bombed Warsaw. The Navy is engaged. Himmler's apparatus is embedded in occupied territory. Removing Hitler now would not end the war — it would require a new government to negotiate an armistice, which France and Britain might or might not accept, and which the German military and public might or might not support. The mechanism for stopping what has been started is no longer as clear as it was in 1938.

And yet: the officer has just watched Warsaw burn. He has the reports of the Einsatzgruppen. He has a general who is willing. He has a contact in Berlin who can move.

The question before the council:

*War has begun. Systematic mass killing of civilians is underway as documented operational fact, not projection. The moral case for removing Hitler is now supported by actual rather than projected atrocity. But the consequentialist mechanism — assassination leads to better outcome — is now genuinely uncertain in ways it was not in 1938. Removing Hitler in October 1939 requires: a successor willing to seek armistice, Allied willingness to grant armistice terms, German military and public willingness to accept them, and the ability to halt an SS apparatus already embedded in occupied territory. Does the strengthened moral case overcome the weakened consequentialist mechanism? And what does it mean morally to know that 3.3 million Polish Jews are being concentrated into ghettos, understand the probable trajectory, and weigh that against the uncertain probability that assassination would actually stop it?*

---

*Historical context: Scenario 4 of 5. The Einsatzgruppen killed approximately 65,000 Polish civilians in the September-October 1939 campaign. Operation Tannenberg targeted approximately 61,000 Polish leaders for elimination. Halder contemplated a coup in the autumn of 1939 but again pulled back — in part because he assessed the German public would not support removing a victorious war leader. The Polish ghettos were established beginning October 1939. The Wannsee Conference formalizing the Final Solution would occur in January 1942. At this waypoint the consequentialist mechanism is genuinely uncertain: historians debate whether a 1939 coup government could have achieved armistice and halted the Holocaust.*
